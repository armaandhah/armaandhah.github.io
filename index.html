Objective

As the only mechanical engineer supporting several product lines at once, my job was to provide mechanical design and integration for whatever the electrical and firmware teams needed next, across a redesigned battery charger, a mobile testbench cart, three phases of an in-house GNSS positioning device, and a sewer-inspection robot's camera gimbal.

Deliverables
  • Redesigned the CRABCAKE sewer-inspection robot's pitch and roll camera gimbal, cutting package volume 53%.
  • Built closed-loop joystick control firmware for the gimbal's two axes.
  • Led mechanical design across three phases of an in-house GNSS positioning device, from an early test fixture through a complete four-board V3/V4 unit.
  • Designed a custom 2s2p battery pack and helped bring up the BMS board.
  • Redesigned the external battery charger enclosure around a new single-slot PCB, with a COTS light pipe, a weighted base, and slip-fit battery retention.
  • Designed and built a Testbench Mobility Unit, a caster cart that interfaces directly with the lab's existing testbench for one-operator transfers.
  • Deployed an automated 3D-printing farm to streamline prototyping across the product line.
The Process
CRABCAKE CAMERA GIMBAL

CRABCAKE caught my attention because I had worked with combat robotics before and wanted to learn the wiring and programming side of a device, not just the mechanical build. Austin had a pitch and roll gimbal prototype that he and Diego started and never finished, so I picked it up from there. The roll shaft runs in a mounted ball bearing sized for a 5/8 inch shaft, then steps down to 9.5mm where it meets the driven gear, and I had to bore a stock 6mm gear out to that size on a drill press since metal gears for a 3mm motor shaft only come in one bore size. Holding the gear in a standard vise let the free end lift while tightening, leaving the bore slightly off center, something I'd fix next time with v-blocks instead.

Gear reduction started as a target around 2 to 1, but part availability and spacing pushed both axes to a final ratio of 1 to 3, 20 teeth driving 60. The slower RPM turned out to be a non-issue, the camera still moved plenty fast while pulling about a watt of power. For the slip ring I reused a 12-channel unit we already had for the motor and encoder rather than buying a smaller one, since fitting the RasPi camera through any slip ring option was either impossible or expensive, so the camera stayed on its own ribbon cable instead.

Control runs off a single joystick. The roll axis spins freely and continuously off the joystick's Y deflection, while the pitch axis is mechanically limited to about 180 degrees and I capped it in software using the pitch encoder's count, with a soft limit and a tighter hard limit set inside the true mechanical stops so the axis can only ever be blocked moving outward, never trapped against a stop. A rezero button lets the operator redefine center whenever the arm is manually returned to true zero. Altogether the redesign cut package volume 53 percent.

POSITIONING CELL INITIATIVE, GNSS DEVICE

The Positioning Cell Initiative was the company's own take on an Emlid-type RTK GNSS device, built after a chip deal with a supplier of IMU and GNSS chips. My job across all three phases was to give the firmware and electrical teams a mechanical platform to test against. Phase 1 had to happen fast, tied to how much internship time my teammates had left, so instead of designing a new platform I modified an existing CRKT 3.1 unit, removing unused features to free up mounting holes and adding a survey pole adapter that raised the antenna off the ground for a cleaner GNSS fix. That adapter used a print-pause 5/8-11 coupling nut as its attachment point, the first time I tried that technique, and it worked cleanly enough that I reused it through the next two phases.

Phase 2 moved testing off the CRKT entirely onto an isolated rig of IMUs, breakout boards, and a dev kit, nicknamed the sceptre. I split it into four pieces for easy assembly and modularity, since supporting multiple IMUs at once was worth the extra design time it would save later in testing. The whole frame held zero offset between the survey pole tip and the IMU and antenna centers in every direction except vertical, which kept the RTK math simple and cut down on firmware setup time.

Phase 3 built the first complete unit, a four-board device (positioning, main, interface, and BMS boards) shaped around the company logo's triangular geometry. I worked board-level design with our PCB designer using Altium Co-Designer to move models back and forth between SolidWorks and Altium, and because the tapered form made a four-board assembly genuinely risky, I wrote out a specific upside-down, bottom-up build sequence so the boards, ribbon cables, and battery pack went in correctly every time. I also drafted the schematic for a custom 2s2p battery pack built from the same cells as the CRKT packs, built the first one with help from the teammate who designs our battery packs, and picked up surface-mount soldering hand-populating chips onto the BMS board myself.

Once V3 proved the concept, I designed a V4 housing that takes the company logo's real proportions seriously, every dimension scales off a single reference length so the proportions hold no matter the overall size. It reuses the same four boards in a tighter arrangement with the battery sandwiched between two of them, trading a rougher assembly sequence for a much more product-ready look.

BATTERY CHARGER ENCLOSURE

The existing charger enclosure charged two CRKT battery packs at once and was already FDM printed in low-volume runs, but the electrical team's move to a single-slot charger board meant the whole enclosure had to be reworked around it. The LED diffuser started as a multicolor print, a two-layer white PETG shell printed straight into the black body, which avoided a separate part entirely. After talking it over with the PCB designer we switched to a commercial light pipe instead for tighter tolerances and better light transmission, and I also left room in the housing for a future multi-LED status array.

The lightweight housing also slid and tipped during normal one-handed use, so I looked at a print-pause weighing system first and dropped it for being needlessly complex, then landed on filling the upper section solid at 100 percent infill for extra low mass, plus corner-bolted rubber feet that clamp the base plate and PCB retention together in one fastening step. Battery retention had the same reliability problem as any friction fit, inconsistent across print batches with no tactile confirmation the pack was seated, so I ran an Excel-driven design table in SolidWorks sweeping the pocket offset and converged on a proper slip fit after 13 iterations.

TESTBENCH MOBILITY UNIT

One of our lab testbenches was fully stationary, which limited where and how we could run tests without risking a heavy lift that could hurt someone or knock sensitive instrumentation out of alignment. I designed a caster cart that interfaces directly with the existing testbench frame, matching its height with custom-cut T-slot aluminum extrusions and reusing its exact rail profile and spacing so a heavy baseplate rolls smoothly from bench to cart without catching. The cart's own T-slot geometry tracks into the bench's open slots as it rolls up, so alignment is self-guiding, and four total-lock casters immobilize both rotation and swivel once it's seated, keeping the whole transfer stable under a single operator. The whole thing had to come together with basic shop tools before the teammate who built the original testbench left, so speed mattered as much as the engineering.

Across all four projects the same few lessons kept repeating. Loop in the electrical and firmware teams early, since most of my mechanical constraints came from them. Design for whoever has to assemble the thing, not just the assembly itself. And write down the reasoning behind a decision, not just the result, since that's what the next person actually needs.

Objective

Combat robotics matches are usually decided by the weapon: it has to survive a direct hit from an opposing 30 lb robot without cracking mid-match. My objective was to design and manufacture a drisk-style spinning weapon for Skull Crusher that maximized destructive energy within a strict 12 lb weapon-mass allocation, while surviving worst-case impact loading.

Deliverables
  • Designed and manufactured a drisk-style spinning weapon system in SolidWorks, iterating geometry and packaging to stay within a 12 lb weapon-mass allocation.
  • Performed ANSYS FEA across multiple design revisions to predict stress concentrations and loading responses, validating spline hub survivability under expected combat impact forces.
  • Machined critical components on manual lathe and mill, and programmed toolpaths in Fusion CAM to manufacture complex parts on a CNC mill.
The Process

A drisk trades some of a full disc's destructive radius for lower rotational inertia and better packaging, which matters a lot at a 12 lb weapon-mass limit shared against armor and drivetrain. Early geometry passes were mostly about finding how much material could come off the disc face before it stopped being worth spinning.

Once a geometry looked viable, it went through ANSYS FEA to check the failure modes that actually matter in a hit: stress concentration around the hub bore and shear through the spline under a worst-case impact load. Objective

The MATE ROV competition tasks teams with building underwater systems that complete mission-specific tasks at depth. As FLOAT Mechanical Lead, my job was to design and build the mechanical platform for a vertical profiling float, a device that dives to a target depth, logs environmental data, and resurfaces, engineering the buoyancy, structure, and waterproofing that let the electrical team's sensors and electronics do their job reliably.

Deliverables
  • Buoyancy engine designed to intake enough water to dive to 3 m depth and resurface reliably.
  • 3D-printed, waterproof housing sized to house the buoyancy engine, battery packs, PCB, wiring, and microcontroller.
  • Custom battery packs designed to fit the housing.
  • Depth-tested the full assembly to confirm zero leakage across 150+ hours submerged.
  • Directed a sub-team through CAD modeling, fabrication, and mechanical testing.
  • Technical documentation covering all design parameters, submitted for competition.
  • Presented the device at the competition design presentation.
The Process

Our program has no machine shop, so every housing component had to come off an FDM printer, a constraint no other team at the MATE World Championship worked within. FDM parts leak at the layer lines under pressure, so getting a print to actually hold water became its own project. Hand-brushing acetone onto the ABS parts blended the layers but was slow and inconsistent, and a small vapor chamber worked well on small parts but smoothed larger housings unevenly. What stuck was a flash vapor chamber sized for the housing itself, which smoothed a part in minutes with consistent results and let us reprint, smooth, and pressure-test a new housing revision in a single day. The housing went through 9 iterations before we landed on one that sealed reliably every time.

For the buoyancy engine we weighed two concepts, a bank of three or four smaller syringes sharing a lead screw, or a single large syringe with the lead screw running inside it. The multi-syringe layout was the most common approach in past competitions, but it meant several seals' worth of friction and a much bigger package, so we went with the single-syringe design for lower friction and a smaller footprint. With next to no budget, the drive motor ended up being a stepper scavenged from an old 3D printer rather than something bought to spec, so the first real question was whether it could do the job at all. I measured plunger friction directly with a force gauge, separating the static breakaway force from the steady kinetic force, combined that with the water-pressure load at depth and a factor of safety of 2, then converted that to a torque requirement through the lead screw's lead and an assumed screw efficiency. The number landed close enough to the motor's rated holding torque to know it would work.

The housing splits into two pieces so the batteries, wiring, and code stay reachable between test runs, which meant two separate waterproof interfaces to design rather than one, the main lid and the pass-through where the syringe tip exits the housing. I built a test puck matching the housing diameter to validate sealing independent of the rest of the assembly. Seals in years past had failed here, so I went back and ran the numbers myself.

Using the standard O-ring compression-ratio formula, I sized the gland depth for a 25% compression on our ring's cross-section, with a groove width at 1.5× that cross-section so the compressed ring had room to spread instead of extruding out the sides, and fillets in every corner to protect the ring. That geometry passed a slow, controlled one-minute depth test and then a fast, choppy depth-change test with zero leakage on both interfaces. I also checked the bolt pattern clamping it down, a 10-bolt M4 pattern, against the required clamping force, landing around a factor of safety of 3, and checked the housing wall itself in hoop stress against a simulated 20 m depth, well past our actual 2.5 to 3 m operating range, which came out around a factor of safety of 12.

The float also needed to survive being set down hard on a pool deck, so I sized a set of TPU feet by working backward from an impact-energy budget, estimating the float's drop velocity from a drag balance, converting that to kinetic energy on impact, and comparing the stopping force over a compliant foot's ~10 mm of give against a rigid ~1 mm contact, which cut the estimated impact force by roughly 10×.
