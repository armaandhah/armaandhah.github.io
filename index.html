Objective

As one of few mechanical engineers supporting several product lines at once, my job was to provide mechanical design and integration for whatever the electrical and firmware teams needed next, across a redesigned battery charger, a mobile testbench cart, three phases of an in-house GNSS positioning device, and a sewer-inspection robot's camera gimbal.

Deliverables
  • Redesigned the CRABCAKE sewer-inspection robot's pitch and roll camera gimbal, cutting package volume 53%.
  • Led mechanical design across three phases of an in-house GNSS positioning device, from an early test fixture through a complete four-board unit.
  • Designed a custom 2s2p battery pack and helped bring up the BMS board.
  • Redesigned the external battery charger enclosure around a new single-slot PCB, with a COTS light pipe, a weighted base, and slip-fit battery retention.
  • Designed and built a Testbench Mobility Unit, a caster cart that interfaces directly with the lab's existing testbench for one-operator transfers.
  • Maintained an automated 3D-printing farm to streamline prototyping across the product line.
  • Recorded the fastest lap time in the company's outing at K1 Speed, a nice side benefit of caring about vehicle dynamics.
The Process
CRABCAKE CAMERA GIMBAL

CRABCAKE caught my attention because I had worked with combat robotics before and wanted to learn the wiring and programming side of a device, not just the mechanical build. I picked up an unfinished pitch and roll gimbal prototype and carried it through to a working redesign. The roll shaft runs in a mounted ball bearing sized for a 5/8 inch shaft, then steps down to 9.5mm where it meets the driven gear, which I bored out from stock on a drill press since metal gears for a 3mm motor shaft only come in one bore size.

I used the motor that was already on hand and landed on a 1 to 3 ratio, 20 teeth driving 60, which kept the camera moving plenty fast while pulling about a watt of power. For the slip ring I chose a 12-channel unit sized to allow a full 360 degrees of continuous roll.

Control runs on a Teensy 4.1 reading a single joystick. The roll axis spins freely and continuously off the joystick's Y deflection, while the pitch axis is mechanically limited to about 180 degrees and capped in software using the pitch encoder's count, with a soft limit and a tighter hard limit set inside the true mechanical stops so the axis can only ever be blocked moving outward, never trapped against a stop. A rezero button lets the operator redefine center whenever the arm is manually returned to true zero. Altogether the redesign cut package volume 53 percent. This was a prototype built to integrate with the sewer crawler platform, and I built the proof of concept for it.

POSITIONING CELL INITIATIVE, GNSS DEVICE

The Positioning Cell Initiative was the company's own RTK GNSS device. My job across all three phases was to give the firmware and electrical teams a mechanical platform to test against. Phase 1 had to happen fast, so instead of designing a new platform I retrofitted an existing CRKT unit for my own testing purposes, a GNSS device currently in production by another company that Advanced Spade was also in the process of engineering. I removed unused features to free up mounting holes and added a survey pole adapter that raised the antenna off the ground for a cleaner GNSS fix. That adapter used a print-pause 5/8-11 coupling nut as its attachment point, a technique I reused through the next two phases.

Phase 2 moved testing off the CRKT entirely onto an isolated rig of IMUs, breakout boards, and a dev kit, nicknamed the sceptre. I split it into four pieces for easy assembly and modularity, since supporting multiple IMUs at once was worth the extra design time it would save later in testing. The whole frame held zero offset between the survey pole tip and the IMU and antenna centers in every direction except vertical, which kept the RTK math simple and cut down on firmware setup time.

Phase 3 built the first complete unit, a four-board device (positioning, main, interface, and BMS boards) shaped around the company logo's triangular geometry. I worked board-level design with our PCB designer using Altium Co-Designer to move models back and forth between SolidWorks and Altium. Because the tapered form made assembly order sensitive, I wrote out a specific upside-down, bottom-up build sequence so the boards, ribbon cables, and battery pack went in correctly every time. I also drafted the schematic for a custom 2s2p battery pack built from the same cells as the CRKT packs, built the first one, and picked up surface-mount soldering hand-populating chips onto the BMS board myself.

BATTERY CHARGER ENCLOSURE

The company needed a battery charger for the CRKT device that the field team could rely on, so I built the enclosure around a new single-slot charger board, adding a commercial light pipe for the status LED, a solid-infill upper section to keep the base weighted, and corner-bolted rubber feet that clamp the base plate and battery retention together in one fastening step. Battery retention itself was tuned through an Excel-driven design table in SolidWorks, sweeping the pocket offset until it converged on a reliable slip fit.

TESTBENCH MOBILITY UNIT

One of our lab testbenches was fully stationary, which limited where and how we could run tests without risking a heavy lift that could hurt someone or knock sensitive instrumentation out of alignment. I designed a caster cart that interfaces directly with the existing testbench frame, matching its height with custom-cut T-slot aluminum extrusions and reusing its exact rail profile and spacing so a heavy baseplate rolls smoothly from bench to cart without catching. The cart's own T-slot geometry tracks into the bench's open slots as it rolls up, so alignment is self-guiding, and four total-lock casters immobilize both rotation and swivel once it's seated, keeping the whole transfer stable under a single operator.

A few lessons repeated across all four projects. Loop in the electrical and firmware teams early, since most of my mechanical constraints came from them. Design for whoever has to assemble the thing, not just the assembly itself. And write down the reasoning behind a decision, not just the result.

U.A.A.O.D.A.D. — 3 lb Combat Robot

Designed, manufactured, and competed with a 3 lb eggbeater combat robot in one week.

CNC Machining
Fusion CAM
Manual Mill & Lathe

FDM 3D Printing

Objective

With just a week until an interschool 3 lb combat robot tournament, my teammate and I decided to sign up anyway. We were both on the Baja SAE suspension subteam, which is where the robot's name came from, Upper A-Arms of Doom and Despair, U.A.A.O.D.A.D. for short, for reasons that should be pretty easy to guess. That left almost no time to plan, design, gather parts, manufacture, assemble, and test a full weapon-class robot, so the goal became getting a functioning eggbeater-style bot to the competition floor with whatever the week allowed.

Deliverables
  • Designed and iterated a parameterized eggbeater weapon geometry, using a weight-versus-energy sweep to size the disc within a 0.7 lb weapon-mass target.
  • Printed a one-piece TPU 98A chassis and CNC-machined a hardened 4140 steel weapon disc.
  • Sized the weapon's pulley and belt drive within our shaft-size constraints, balancing gear ratio against spin-up time.
  • Bandsawed and CNC-machined UHMW uprights and a top plate from printed DXF templates after the waterjet went down.
The Process
WEAPON ENERGY SIZING

With the tournament a week out, our first priority was locking down a weapon design fast enough to leave time for manufacturing. One of our weapon goals was a minimum of 300 J of stored energy, so we ran a batch of geometry iterations on a simple parameterized eggbeater profile, sweeping length and width and logging the resulting moment of inertia and rotational energy for each combination against a set battery voltage and motor RPM. Plotting those points against weapon weight showed a roughly linear relationship between mass and stored energy, which let us pick a geometry that cleared the 300 J target with room to spare while keeping the disc itself under 0.7 lb, leaving the rest of the 3 lb budget for the chassis, drivetrain, and electronics. We ran the same kind of sweep on battery and motor sizing, checking voltage and KV combinations against the target RPM to get as much power into the system as our weight and cost limits would allow.

MATERIALS

For the chassis we printed the entire shell as a single piece in TPU 98A, one of the more rigid TPUs available, since we needed real impact toughness without the assembly time a multi-piece design would have cost us that week. For the weapon itself we went with hardened 4140 steel over the annealed version or a lighter aluminum, trading easier machining for the wear resistance and toughness needed to survive repeated hits at full RPM without the edge deforming or cracking, then took on cutting that hardened stock ourselves on a CNC mill with the limited tooling we had access to.

POWERTRAIN

The weapon runs a 20-tooth pulley on a 1/4 inch shaft driven by a 14-tooth pulley on the motor's 1/8 inch shaft, a 1.429 to 1 ratio set largely by which shaft and pulley sizes we could actually get within a week. That ratio put us at a calculated spin-up time of about 1.7 seconds to reach a max weapon speed of roughly 19,600 rpm, and it was the tradeoff we kept running into everywhere we pushed the ratio for more torque, spin-up time crept up, and everywhere we pushed it for a faster spin-up, we gave up weapon energy at the hit.

UPRIGHTS & TOP PLATE

The uprights and top plate were UHMW, chosen for its impact resistance and low weight relative to aluminum, since neither part needed to carry much load beyond holding the weapon shaft in place and keeping the assembly square. Our waterjet was down that week, so we printed DXF templates, cut the upright and top plate profiles by hand on a vertical bandsaw, then finished the shaft bores and mounting holes on the CNC mill.

WIRING & THE FINAL PUSH

With about 12 hours left before competition morning, we finally had all our motors, ESCs, and the battery in hand, which left very little time to plan out wiring and soldering, let alone program the ESCs. I harnessed the entire robot from scratch over the next few hours, and the time crunch pushed my soldering "skills" well past where they started the week. We were also still making design-for-assembly changes to the robot at that point, since a few interfaces just hadn't been fully worked out yet and there wasn't time left to flesh them out properly.

Results & Lessons Learned

We built a fully functioning 3 lb combat robot from scratch in a single week, which we're proud of on its own. Where we fell short was competition day itself. The ESCs never got fully programmed in the time we had, so we achieved a successful weapon spinup but had no meaningful control over it once it was running.

Working under that kind of pressure meant leaning on a wide mix of skills at once, CAD, CAM, machining, and harnessing, often in the same afternoon, and learning fast which tools we actually had access to versus which ones we didn't, then building the plan around that instead of losing time to it. The housing came out bulkier than it needed to be and wasn't designed with assembly in mind, which is the first thing we'd fix on a second pass. We're hoping to come back next year with a more competitive, more compact version of this robot.

Objective

Flagship Combat Robotics competes in the heavier weight classes under UCLA ASME, including Carnage at the Cube. My focus within the team was the weapon system for Skull Crusher, our 30 lb combat robot, which had to survive direct hits from an opposing 30 lb robot while staying inside a strict 12 lb weapon-mass allocation.

Deliverables
  • Took the weapon system from an initial design through iteration to a final, competition-ready version, adapting it for clearances and optimizing for weight while keeping the original performance goals in mind.
  • Machined the spline hub, weapon shaft, motor shaft, and pulleys, learning Fusion CAM to program the spline hub on the CNC mill, then assembled the weapon and performed on-the-fly maintenance between fights.
The Process

I worked in a four-person subteam to bring the weapon system from an initial design to its final, competition-ready form. My main contribution was taking that initial design through iteration, adapting it for clearances and trimming weight out of the drisk and hub so the assembly hit its speed target without breaking the 12 lb weapon-mass allocation, all while keeping the original performance goals in mind.

On the manufacturing side I machined the spline hub on a CNC mill, learning Fusion CAM to program the toolpaths, along with the weapon shaft, the motor shaft, and the pulleys. Every part of the weapon, from the hub to the shaft, was designed to come apart quickly, so a damaged piece could be pulled and swapped for a spare between rounds instead of taking the robot out of the competition.

The finished weapon spun up to 10,000 rpm and held up through our comp fights with minimal weapon-side maintenance, mostly swapping in a fresh set of blades between rounds. The robot placed third out of twelve teams.

Objective

The MATE ROV competition tasks teams with building underwater systems that complete mission-specific tasks at depth. As FLOAT Mechanical Lead, my job was to design and build the mechanical platform for a vertical profiling float, a device that dives to a target depth, logs environmental data, and resurfaces, engineering the buoyancy, structure, and waterproofing that let the electrical team's sensors and electronics do their job reliably.

Deliverables
  • Buoyancy engine designed to intake enough water to dive to 3 m depth and resurface reliably.
  • 3D-printed, waterproof housing sized to house the buoyancy engine, battery packs, PCB, wiring, and microcontroller.
  • Custom battery packs designed to fit the housing.
  • Depth-tested the full assembly to confirm zero leakage across 150+ hours submerged.
  • Directed a sub-team through CAD modeling, fabrication, and mechanical testing.
  • Technical documentation covering all design parameters, submitted for competition.
  • Presented the device at the competition design presentation.
The Process

Our program has no machine shop, so every housing component had to come off an FDM printer, a constraint no other team at the MATE World Championship worked within. FDM parts leak at the layer lines under pressure, so getting a print to actually hold water became its own project. Hand-brushing acetone onto the ABS parts blended the layers but was slow and inconsistent, and a small vapor chamber worked well on small parts but smoothed larger housings unevenly. What stuck was a flash vapor chamber sized for the housing itself, which smoothed a part in minutes with consistent results and let us reprint, smooth, and pressure-test a new housing revision in a single day. The housing went through 9 iterations before we landed on one that sealed reliably every time.

For the buoyancy engine we weighed two concepts, a bank of three or four smaller syringes sharing a lead screw, or a single large syringe with the lead screw running inside it. The multi-syringe layout was the most common approach in past competitions, but it meant several seals' worth of friction and a much bigger package, so we went with the single-syringe design for lower friction and a smaller footprint. With next to no budget, the drive motor ended up being a stepper scavenged from an old 3D printer rather than something bought to spec, so the first real question was whether it could do the job at all. I measured plunger friction directly with a force gauge, separating the static breakaway force from the steady kinetic force, combined that with the water-pressure load at depth and a factor of safety of 2, then converted that to a torque requirement through the lead screw's lead and an assumed screw efficiency. The number landed close enough to the motor's rated holding torque to know it would work.

The housing splits into two pieces so the batteries, wiring, and code stay reachable between test runs, which meant two separate waterproof interfaces to design rather than one, the main lid and the pass-through where the syringe tip exits the housing. I built a test puck matching the housing diameter to validate sealing independent of the rest of the assembly. Seals in years past had failed here, so I went back and ran the numbers myself.

Using the standard O-ring compression-ratio formula, I sized the gland depth for a 25% compression on our ring's cross-section, with a groove width at 1.5× that cross-section so the compressed ring had room to spread instead of extruding out the sides, and fillets in every corner to protect the ring. That geometry passed a slow, controlled one-minute depth test and then a fast, choppy depth-change test with zero leakage on both interfaces. I also checked the bolt pattern clamping it down, a 10-bolt M4 pattern, against the required clamping force, landing around a factor of safety of 3, and checked the housing wall itself in hoop stress against a simulated 20 m depth, well past our actual 2.5 to 3 m operating range, which came out around a factor of safety of 12.

The float also needed to survive being set down hard on a pool deck, so I sized a set of TPU feet by working backward from an impact-energy budget, estimating the float's drop velocity from a drag balance, converting that to kinetic energy on impact, and comparing the stopping force over a compliant foot's ~10 mm of give against a rigid ~1 mm contact, which cut the estimated impact force by roughly 10×.
